Name your state. Get your state's deadline.

How long a debt collector can legally come after you changes at the state line. This is every state, in years. Find yours, know your clock.

  • Rule one. The state that can sue you sets the clock. Not the state named in your agreement. Settled case law: Portfolio Recovery Associates, LLC v. King, 14 N.Y.3d 410 (2010).
  • Rule two. The clock starts at the first missed payment. A payment or written acknowledgment usually restarts it; the Watch column marks the states that bar that restart on consumer debt.
  • Rule three. Credit cards are the fight. Some states count them as written contracts, some as open accounts, a few have never settled it. Where the collector cannot produce the cardholder agreement, the shorter period usually wins.

Confirm any number against your state's own code before it goes in a letter or a court answer. Federal student loans have no deadline at all. This sheet is education, not legal advice.

Why the number matters. A debt past your state's deadline is time-barred. The collector can still call and still report, but they cannot sue you or threaten to sue you over it. A collector that threatens suit on a time-barred debt is making a false representation (15 USC 1692e(2)(J)), and federal rule 12 CFR 1006.26 puts that in writing for debt collectors. Know your state's number and you know when the threat is empty.
